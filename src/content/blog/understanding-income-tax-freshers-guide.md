# Understanding Income Tax: A Fresher's Guide

Congratulations! You've landed your first job, and the excitement of financial independence is just
setting in. Along with that first salary credit comes a new reality that every working professional
must face—income tax. For many freshers, the world of taxation feels like learning a foreign
language. Terms like TDS, HRA, 80C, Form 16, and ITR can seem overwhelming at first.

But here's the truth: understanding income tax isn't as complicated as it seems. Once you grasp the
basics, you'll not only save money through legal deductions but also become financially smarter and
more responsible. This comprehensive guide is designed specifically for freshers like you who are
navigating the tax landscape for the first time.

By the end of this guide, you'll understand how income tax works in India, how to plan your taxes
wisely, and how to file your returns without stress. Let's demystify income tax together.

## What is Income Tax?

### The Basics

Income tax is a direct tax levied by the Government of India on the income earned by individuals,
companies, and other entities. Unlike indirect taxes (like GST) that you pay on purchases, income
tax is paid directly on your earnings.

The legal framework for income tax in India is governed by the Income Tax Act, 1961. The rules and
rates are updated each year through the Finance Bill presented during the Union Budget.

### Why Does the Government Collect Taxes?

Before feeling frustrated about parting with your hard-earned money, it helps to understand where
your taxes go:

**Infrastructure Development**: Roads, railways, airports, and public transportation are funded
largely through tax revenues.

**Defense and Security**: Protecting the nation's borders and maintaining law and order requires
significant financial resources.

**Healthcare and Education**: Government hospitals, schools, and scholarship programs are supported
by tax money.

**Social Welfare Programs**: Schemes like MGNREGA, pension programs, and food subsidies help
millions of Indians.

**Public Services**: From street lights to garbage collection, taxes fund the services we often take
for granted.

When you pay taxes, you're contributing to the nation's development. It's a responsibility that
comes with earning an income.

### Who Needs to Pay Income Tax?

In India, any individual whose total income exceeds the basic exemption limit is liable to pay
income tax. The basic exemption limit varies:

- Under the New Tax Regime: ₹3,00,000 per year (₹25,000 per month)
- Under the Old Tax Regime: ₹2,50,000 per year (₹20,833 per month)

If your income is below these thresholds, you're not required to pay income tax, though you may
still need to file a return in certain situations.

## Why Should You Care About Income Tax?

As a fresher, you might think tax planning is something for older, higher-earning professionals.
That's a common misconception. Here's why you should care right from your first salary:

### 1. Legal Obligation

Tax evasion is a punishable offense in India. Failing to pay taxes or file returns when required can
result in:

- Penalties and fines
- Interest on unpaid taxes
- Legal prosecution in severe cases
- Problems with future financial activities

### 2. Saving Money

Proper tax planning can save you a significant amount of money each year. A fresher earning ₹6 lakhs
annually could save anywhere from ₹20,000 to ₹50,000 or more through legal deductions and smart
planning.

Those savings compound over time. Money saved through tax planning can be invested for your future.

### 3. Building Good Habits

The financial habits you develop early in your career tend to stick. Starting with good tax planning
habits at 22 means you'll be a financially savvy investor by 30.

### 4. Loan and Credit Applications

When you apply for loans or credit cards, banks often ask for ITR (Income Tax Return) documents.
Consistent filing builds a credible financial history.

### 5. Visa Applications

Many countries require proof of income and tax compliance when processing visa applications. Filed
ITRs serve as evidence of legitimate income.

### 6. Understanding Your Actual Worth

Until you understand taxation, you don't truly understand your finances. Knowing the difference
between gross salary, in-hand salary, and post-tax income helps you make better financial decisions.

## Understanding Your Salary Structure

Before we dive into tax calculations, you need to understand how your salary is structured. Most
employers break down your CTC (Cost to Company) into multiple components:

### Common Salary Components

**1. Basic Salary**

Your basic salary is the core of your compensation and typically forms 40-50% of your CTC. It's
fully taxable. Many other components (like HRA and PF) are calculated as percentages of basic
salary.

**Why it matters**: A higher basic salary increases your take-home pay but also increases your tax
liability since basic salary is fully taxable.

**2. House Rent Allowance (HRA)**

HRA is provided to help you pay rent if you're living in rented accommodation. It's partially exempt
from tax if you actually pay rent.

The exempt portion is the minimum of:

- Actual HRA received
- 50% of basic salary (for metros) or 40% (for non-metros)
- Actual rent paid minus 10% of basic salary

**Example**: If your basic salary is ₹30,000/month, HRA is ₹15,000, and you pay ₹12,000 rent in
Bangalore:

- Actual HRA = ₹15,000
- 50% of basic = ₹15,000
- Rent paid - 10% of basic = ₹12,000 - ₹3,000 = ₹9,000

The exempt amount is ₹9,000 (minimum of the three). You'll be taxed on ₹6,000.

**3. Dearness Allowance (DA)**

More common in government jobs, DA is meant to offset inflation. It's fully taxable.

**4. Special Allowance**

This is a catch-all category for any amount not covered under other heads. It's fully taxable.

**5. Conveyance/Transport Allowance**

An allowance for commuting to work. While previously exempt up to a limit, it's now generally
taxable unless you have actual transport-related expenses.

**6. Leave Travel Allowance (LTA)**

Money provided for domestic travel during leave. Tax exempt if you actually travel domestically and
submit bills. Only two journeys in a block of four years are exempt.

**7. Medical Allowance**

Some employers provide a medical allowance. It's fully taxable under current rules, though medical
insurance premiums remain deductible.

**8. Employer's Provident Fund Contribution**

Your employer contributes 12% of your basic salary to your Provident Fund account. This isn't shown
in your take-home pay but is part of your CTC and grows tax-free.

**9. Gratuity**

A retirement benefit payable after completing five years of service. A portion of your CTC is set
aside for this, though you only receive it when you leave.

**10. Bonus/Variable Pay**

Many companies offer performance-linked bonuses. These are fully taxable in the year you receive
them.

### Sample Salary Breakup

Let's look at a typical fresher salary of ₹6,00,000 CTC:

| Component         | Monthly Amount | Annual Amount |
| ----------------- | -------------- | ------------- |
| Basic Salary      | ₹20,000        | ₹2,40,000     |
| HRA               | ₹10,000        | ₹1,20,000     |
| Special Allowance | ₹8,500         | ₹1,02,000     |
| Medical Allowance | ₹1,250         | ₹15,000       |
| LTA               | ₹833           | ₹10,000       |
| Employer PF       | ₹2,400         | ₹28,800       |
| Variable Pay      | ₹7,017         | ₹84,200       |
| **Total CTC**     | **₹50,000**    | **₹6,00,000** |

Your actual in-hand salary (before tax) would be lower, typically around ₹35,000-40,000 after PF
contributions and tax deductions.

## Tax Regimes: Old vs. New

Taxpayers in India can choose between two tax regimes. Understanding both is crucial for effective
tax planning.

### The Old Tax Regime

The old regime has been around for decades. It has higher tax rates but allows you to claim numerous
deductions and exemptions:

**Key Features**:

- Higher tax rates (starts at 5% for income above ₹2.5 lakhs)
- Multiple deductions available (80C, 80D, HRA, etc.)
- Exemptions on various allowances
- Better for those who have significant deductible expenses

### The New Tax Regime

Introduced as the default option, the new regime offers lower tax rates but removes most deductions:

**Key Features**:

- Lower tax rates across all slabs
- Very few deductions available (only employer NPS contribution)
- No exemptions on HRA, LTA, etc.
- Simpler to understand and calculate
- Better for those with few deductions or no home loans/rent

### Which One Should You Choose?

**Choose the Old Regime if**:

- You pay significant rent and can claim HRA exemption
- You have home loan interest to claim
- You invest in tax-saving instruments like ELSS, PPF, NPS
- You pay health insurance premiums for family
- Your deductions exceed ₹1.5-2 lakhs annually

**Choose the New Regime if**:

- You don't have many deductible expenses
- You live with parents (no rent)
- You prefer simplicity over complex calculations
- You're just starting out and haven't built investment habits
- Your total deductions are less than ₹1.5 lakhs

**Important**: You can switch between regimes each year when filing your return (with some
conditions for business income).

## Income Tax Slabs (Current Rates)

Understanding tax slabs is essential for calculating your tax liability. Here are the current slabs:

### New Tax Regime (Default)

| Income Range             | Tax Rate |
| ------------------------ | -------- |
| Up to ₹3,00,000          | Nil      |
| ₹3,00,001 to ₹7,00,000   | 5%       |
| ₹7,00,001 to ₹10,00,000  | 10%      |
| ₹10,00,001 to ₹12,00,000 | 15%      |
| ₹12,00,001 to ₹15,00,000 | 20%      |
| Above ₹15,00,000         | 30%      |

**Rebate under Section 87A**: If your taxable income is up to ₹7,00,000, your entire tax liability
is rebated. Effectively, you pay zero tax.

### Old Tax Regime

| Income Range            | Tax Rate |
| ----------------------- | -------- |
| Up to ₹2,50,000         | Nil      |
| ₹2,50,001 to ₹5,00,000  | 5%       |
| ₹5,00,001 to ₹10,00,000 | 20%      |
| Above ₹10,00,000        | 30%      |

**Rebate under Section 87A**: If your taxable income is up to ₹5,00,000, you get a rebate of up to
₹12,500.

### Additional Surcharges and Cess

On top of the basic tax, you'll also pay:

**Health and Education Cess**: 4% of total tax payable

**Surcharge** (for higher incomes):

- 10% if income exceeds ₹50 lakhs
- 15% if income exceeds ₹1 crore
- 25% if income exceeds ₹2 crores
- 37% if income exceeds ₹5 crores

For most freshers, the surcharge won't apply, but remember to include the 4% cess in your
calculations.

## Understanding TDS (Tax Deducted at Source)

TDS is probably the first encounter you'll have with the tax system. Before you even see your
salary, a portion might already be deducted towards your tax liability.

### What is TDS?

TDS stands for Tax Deducted at Source. Your employer deducts an estimated amount of tax from your
salary each month and pays it to the government on your behalf. This ensures regular tax collection
and prevents year-end surprises for taxpayers.

### How is TDS Calculated?

At the beginning of each financial year (or when you join), your employer projects your annual
income based on your salary components. They then calculate the estimated tax liability and divide
it by 12 to determine monthly TDS.

**Example**:

- Annual Taxable Income: ₹6,00,000
- Tax under New Regime: ₹15,000 + cess = ₹15,600
- Monthly TDS: ₹15,600 ÷ 12 = ₹1,300

### Investment Declaration

Most companies ask you to declare your planned investments and expenses at the start of the
financial year. Based on this declaration, they reduce your TDS accordingly.

**Common declarations include**:

- Expected rent payments (for HRA exemption)
- Planned investments under 80C
- Health insurance premiums (80D)
- Home loan interest
- Previous employment income (if you've switched jobs)

**Important Note**: You must provide proof of these investments/expenses later. If you don't invest
as declared, your employer will deduct additional TDS later in the year.

### Form 26AS and TDS Verification

Form 26AS is your Annual Tax Statement, available on the Income Tax portal. It shows:

- All TDS deducted during the year
- Advance tax or self-assessment tax paid
- Refunds received

Always verify that TDS deducted by your employer matches what's reflected in Form 26AS.
Discrepancies should be reported to your HR/payroll team.

## Key Deductions Under the Old Regime

If you opt for the old tax regime, understanding deductions is crucial. Here are the major ones:

### Section 80C: The Heavyweight (₹1.5 lakh limit)

Section 80C offers the largest deduction and covers numerous investment and expense categories:

**1. Employee Provident Fund (EPF)** Your 12% contribution to EPF (deducted from basic salary)
qualifies under 80C. For a ₹25,000 basic salary, that's ₹36,000 annually—almost 25% of your 80C
limit.

**2. Public Provident Fund (PPF)** A 15-year savings scheme with government-backed returns. Safe,
tax-free, but locked for a long duration.

**3. Equity Linked Savings Scheme (ELSS)** Mutual funds with a 3-year lock-in period. Offers
potentially higher returns with market-linked risk. The shortest lock-in among 80C instruments.

**4. National Pension System (NPS)** Retirement savings scheme with an additional ₹50,000 deduction
under 80CCD(1B), over and above 80C limits.

**5. Life Insurance Premiums** Premiums paid for life insurance policies covering self, spouse, or
children qualify.

**6. Sukanya Samriddhi Yojana** For those with daughters—a girl child savings scheme with attractive
interest rates.

**7. Tax-Saving Fixed Deposits** 5-year fixed deposits with banks that qualify for tax benefits.
Safe but lower returns.

**8. Tuition Fees** Tuition fees paid for children's education (maximum two children) qualify.

**9. Principal Repayment on Home Loan** The principal portion of your home loan EMI qualifies under
80C.

### Section 80D: Health Insurance (₹25,000 - ₹1 lakh)

Premiums paid for health insurance policies:

| For Whom                    | Maximum Deduction |
| --------------------------- | ----------------- |
| Self, Spouse, Children      | ₹25,000           |
| Parents (below 60)          | ₹25,000           |
| Parents (60 or above)       | ₹50,000           |
| Self (60+) + Senior Parents | Up to ₹1,00,000   |

**Tip for freshers**: If your employer provides health insurance, you might not need a separate
policy. But covering parents (especially if they're seniors) can give significant tax benefits.

### Section 80E: Education Loan Interest

If you took an education loan for your higher studies:

- Interest paid is fully deductible (no upper limit)
- Deduction available for 8 years from when you start repaying
- Loan must be from a recognized financial institution

### Section 80G: Donations

Donations to approved charitable organizations provide deductions:

- 100% deduction for certain funds (PM Relief Fund, etc.)
- 50% deduction for other approved charities
- Subject to qualifying limits

### Section 80TTA: Savings Account Interest (₹10,000)

Interest earned from savings bank accounts (up to ₹10,000) is deductible. Most freshers easily hit
this with their salary account interest.

### Section 24: Home Loan Interest (₹2 lakh)

Interest paid on home loans for self-occupied property is deductible up to ₹2 lakhs. This is a
significant benefit for those with home loans.

## Tax-Free Allowances and Perks

Several salary components and perks are partially or fully tax-free:

### 1. House Rent Allowance (HRA)

As discussed earlier, HRA is exempt to the extent of:

- Actual HRA received
- 50% of basic (metros) or 40% (non-metros)
- Rent paid - 10% of basic salary

**Requirements for claiming HRA exemption**:

- You must actually pay rent
- The house should not be owned by you
- You'll need rent receipts as proof

**If you live with parents**: You can pay rent to your parents (if they own the house) and claim HRA
exemption. Your parents will need to show this rental income, but it might still be tax-efficient
overall.

### 2. Leave Travel Allowance (LTA)

Tax exempt if:

- Used for domestic travel
- Travel is undertaken during leave
- Proof of travel (tickets) is submitted
- Only two journeys exempt in a block of four years

The exempt amount covers only fare (not hotels, food, etc.) for the shortest route.

### 3. Food Coupons/Meal Cards

Meal vouchers (like Sodexo) are tax-free up to ₹50 per meal (₹2,200 per month for 22 working days).

### 4. Company-Provided Car

If your company provides a car for official and personal use:

- A nominal amount is taxed as a perquisite
- Much cheaper than owning a car yourself

### 5. Internet/Phone Reimbursements

When used for official purposes, internet and phone bill reimbursements are tax-free.

### 6. Gifts from Employer

Gifts up to ₹5,000 per year are tax-free.

### 7. Gratuity (When Received)

Gratuity received from a covered employer is exempt up to ₹20 lakhs when you leave (after 5+ years
of service).

## How to Calculate Your Tax Liability

Let's walk through a complete tax calculation example.

### Case Study: Priya, a Fresher with ₹7 LPA CTC

**Priya's Annual Salary Breakup**: | Component | Annual Amount | |-----------|---------------| |
Basic Salary | ₹2,80,000 | | HRA | ₹1,40,000 | | Special Allowance | ₹1,20,000 | | LTA | ₹20,000 | |
Medical Allowance | ₹15,000 | | Employer PF | ₹33,600 | | Variable Pay | ₹91,400 | | **Total CTC** |
**₹7,00,000** |

**Priya's Situation**:

- Lives in a rented apartment in Bangalore, pays ₹14,000/month rent
- Has health insurance through company
- Contributes to EPF (employee contribution: ₹33,600)
- Invests ₹50,000 in ELSS mutual funds
- Pays parents' health insurance: ₹22,000/year

### Calculation Under Old Regime

**Step 1: Calculate Gross Taxable Salary**

| Component         | Amount    | Taxable?              |
| ----------------- | --------- | --------------------- |
| Basic Salary      | ₹2,80,000 | Fully taxable         |
| HRA               | ₹1,40,000 | Partially exempt\*    |
| Special Allowance | ₹1,20,000 | Fully taxable         |
| LTA               | ₹20,000   | Exempt if claimed\*\* |
| Medical Allowance | ₹15,000   | Fully taxable         |
| Variable Pay      | ₹91,400   | Fully taxable         |

\*HRA Exemption Calculation:

- Actual HRA = ₹1,40,000
- 50% of Basic = ₹1,40,000
- Rent - 10% of Basic = (₹14,000 × 12) - ₹28,000 = ₹1,40,000

Exempt HRA = ₹1,40,000 (minimum of three) Taxable HRA = ₹0

**Assuming LTA is claimed with travel: Exempt**

**Gross Taxable Salary** = ₹2,80,000 + ₹0 + ₹1,20,000 + ₹0 + ₹15,000 + ₹91,400 = ₹5,06,400

**Step 2: Apply Deductions**

| Deduction                        | Amount        |
| -------------------------------- | ------------- |
| Section 80C - EPF                | ₹33,600       |
| Section 80C - ELSS               | ₹50,000       |
| Section 80D - Parents' Insurance | ₹22,000       |
| Section 80TTA - Bank Interest    | ₹5,000        |
| **Total Deductions**             | **₹1,10,600** |

**Step 3: Calculate Taxable Income**

Taxable Income = ₹5,06,400 - ₹1,10,600 = ₹3,95,800

**Step 4: Apply Tax Slabs (Old Regime)**

- Up to ₹2,50,000: Nil
- ₹2,50,001 to ₹3,95,800: 5% of ₹1,45,800 = ₹7,290

**Total Tax = ₹7,290**

Since taxable income is under ₹5 lakhs, Section 87A rebate applies. **Tax payable after rebate =
₹0**

### Calculation Under New Regime

**Gross Salary (No exemptions for HRA, LTA)** = ₹2,80,000 + ₹1,40,000 + ₹1,20,000 + ₹20,000 +
₹15,000 + ₹91,400 = ₹6,66,400

**Deductions**: Standard deduction of ₹75,000 (available in new regime)

**Taxable Income** = ₹6,66,400 - ₹75,000 = ₹5,91,400

**Tax Calculation** (New Regime):

- Up to ₹3,00,000: Nil
- ₹3,00,001 to ₹5,91,400: 5% of ₹2,91,400 = ₹14,570

Since taxable income is under ₹7 lakhs, Section 87A rebate applies. **Tax payable after rebate =
₹0**

### Verdict for Priya

Under both regimes, Priya pays ₹0 tax due to rebate provisions. However, as her salary grows, the
old regime might become more beneficial due to deductions.

## Tax Planning Strategies for Freshers

As a fresher, here's a year-by-year approach to tax planning:

### Year 1: Foundation

**1. Understand Your Salary Structure** Ask HR to explain each component of your salary.
Understanding what's taxable helps you plan.

**2. Open Essential Accounts**

- PPF account (₹500 minimum to keep active)
- NPS account (optional, for additional 80CCD(1B) benefit)
- Investment account with a broker for ELSS

**3. Start Small** Don't feel pressured to invest ₹1.5 lakhs immediately. Start with your EPF
contribution and maybe ₹10,000-20,000 in ELSS.

**4. Claim What You Can**

- Claim HRA if paying rent
- Submit actual rent receipts
- Declare savings account interest for 80TTA

### Year 2: Build

**1. Increase Investments** As you're more comfortable with your budget, increase 80C investments.

**2. Consider Health Insurance for Parents** It's often affordable and provides 80D benefits.

**3. Start an SIP** Systematic Investment Plans in ELSS provide tax benefits and build investment
habits.

### Year 3 and Beyond: Optimize

**1. Max Out 80C** Try to utilize the full ₹1.5 lakh limit through a mix of instruments.

**2. Consider NPS for Additional Benefits** The extra ₹50,000 deduction under 80CCD(1B) is valuable
for higher tax brackets.

**3. Plan Major Expenses** If you're considering education, home purchase, or marriage, factor in
the tax implications.

### Common Tax-Saving Instruments Comparison

| Instrument     | Lock-in          | Returns                  | Risk     | Best For                      |
| -------------- | ---------------- | ------------------------ | -------- | ----------------------------- |
| EPF            | Until retirement | ~8.25%                   | Very Low | Everyone with salary          |
| PPF            | 15 years         | ~7.1%                    | Very Low | Long-term safe savings        |
| ELSS           | 3 years          | Variable (market-linked) | Moderate | Young investors               |
| NPS            | Until 60         | Variable                 | Moderate | Additional retirement corpus  |
| Tax-Saving FD  | 5 years          | ~6-7%                    | Very Low | Conservative investors        |
| Life Insurance | Varies           | Low                      | Very Low | Only if needed for protection |

## Understanding Form 16

Form 16 is the most important tax document you'll receive from your employer. It's essentially a
certificate of TDS deduction.

### Parts of Form 16

**Part A**: Contains details of TDS deducted and deposited by your employer. It includes:

- Your name and PAN
- Employer's name and TAN
- Quarter-wise TDS details
- Total TDS for the year

**Part B**: A detailed salary breakup showing:

- Gross salary
- Allowances and exemptions
- Deductions claimed
- Net taxable income
- Tax calculated and deducted

### When Do You Get Form 16?

Employers must issue Form 16 by June 15th each year for the previous financial year.

### Using Form 16 for Filing Returns

Form 16 makes ITR filing straightforward:

- Most information is pre-filled if you use the official portal
- You can verify details against Form 16
- Additional income sources need to be added separately

## How to File Your Income Tax Return (ITR)

Filing your ITR might seem daunting, but it's quite straightforward once you know the process.

### Step 1: Gather Required Documents

Before starting, collect:

- Form 16 from employer
- Bank statements (for interest income)
- Investment proofs (for 80C, 80D claims)
- Form 26AS (from the IT portal)
- Rent receipts (if claiming HRA)
- Home loan statement (if applicable)
- Aadhaar and PAN

### Step 2: Choose the Right ITR Form

For most salaried freshers:

- **ITR-1 (Sahaj)**: If your total income is below ₹50 lakhs and you have salary, one house
  property, and other sources (interest, etc.)

- **ITR-2**: If you have capital gains, more than one house property, or income from abroad

### Step 3: Log into the Portal

1. Visit [incometax.gov.in](https://incometax.gov.in)
2. Log in with your PAN (first-time users need to register)
3. Go to "File Income Tax Return"
4. Select the appropriate Assessment Year
5. Choose Online filing mode

### Step 4: Fill in Details

The portal pre-fills much of the information. Verify:

- Personal details (name, address, etc.)
- Form 16 details (salary, TDS)
- Bank account information
- Additional income sources

**Add missing information**:

- Other income (freelancing, interest, etc.)
- Deductions not reflected
- Capital gains if any

### Step 5: Verify Tax Calculation

The portal calculates your tax liability. Compare with:

- TDS already deducted (should match Form 26AS)
- Any refund due or additional tax payable

### Step 6: Submit and Verify

After submission, verify your return within 30 days using:

- Aadhaar OTP (easiest)
- Net banking
- DSC (Digital Signature Certificate)
- Physical ITR-V sent to CPC Bangalore

### Step 7: Track Your Return

After submission, you can track processing status, check for refunds, and respond to any notices.

### Important Deadlines

| Activity                | Deadline      |
| ----------------------- | ------------- |
| ITR Filing (no audit)   | July 31st     |
| ITR Filing (if belated) | December 31st |
| Revising a filed return | December 31st |

### What If You Miss the Deadline?

- You can file a belated return until December 31st
- Late fee of ₹5,000 applies (₹1,000 if income is below ₹5 lakhs)
- You may lose certain benefits (carry forward of losses)

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

### 1. Not Filing Because "No Tax is Due"

Even if your tax liability is zero, filing is beneficial:

- Creates a financial record
- Helps in loan and visa applications
- Required if TDS was deducted
- Necessary to claim refunds

### 2. Ignoring Other Income Sources

Freshers often forget:

- Interest from savings accounts
- Fixed deposit interest
- Freelancing income
- Gifts above ₹50,000 from non-relatives
- Cryptocurrency gains

### 3. Not Matching Form 16 with 26AS

Discrepancies can lead to notices. Always verify.

### 4. Investing at the Last Minute

Panic investing in March often leads to suboptimal choices. Plan throughout the year.

### 5. Buying Insurance Just for Tax Savings

Don't buy investment + insurance combo products (endowment, ULIPs) just for 80C. They typically
offer poor returns. If you need insurance, buy term insurance. For investment, choose ELSS or PPF.

### 6. Not Keeping Records

Maintain records for:

- Rent receipts
- Investment proofs
- Medical bills
- Other deduction documents

Keep these for at least 6-7 years from the end of the relevant assessment year.

### 7. Choosing the Wrong Tax Regime

Calculate under both regimes before choosing. A wrong choice can cost you money.

### 8. Providing Incorrect Details

PAN, Aadhaar, and bank account details must be accurate. Mistakes can delay refunds or cause other
issues.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Basic Questions

**Q: I joined in October. Do I need to file ITR for the partial year?** A: If your income for
October-March (plus any income earlier) exceeds the basic exemption limit, yes. Otherwise, you still
may want to file for documentation purposes.

**Q: My total income is less than ₹2.5 lakhs. Do I need to file?** A: Mandatory filing isn't
required, but it's recommended for building a financial record, especially if TDS was deducted.

**Q: Can I switch between old and new regime every year?** A: Yes, salaried individuals without
business income can choose each year when filing their return.

**Q: What if I made a mistake in my filed return?** A: You can file a revised return before the end
of the assessment year.

### TDS Questions

**Q: My employer deducted too much TDS. What do I do?** A: File your ITR correctly. The excess TDS
will be refunded to your bank account.

**Q: I switched jobs mid-year. How does TDS work?** A: Provide your Form 12B (from the previous
employer) to your new employer so they can consider previous TDS when calculating current
deductions.

**Q: TDS was deducted but not showing in Form 26AS. What now?** A: Contact your employer's payroll
team. They need to file/correct the TDS return.

### Deduction Questions

**Q: Can I claim HRA if I own a house in another city?** A: Yes, if you're living in a rented house
due to work location, you can claim HRA even if you own a house elsewhere.

**Q: Are my parents' medical bills deductible?** A: Medical bills are not deductible, but health
insurance premiums for parents are (under 80D).

**Q: I took a car loan. Is interest deductible?** A: No, interest on personal or car loans is not
deductible. Only home loan and education loan interest qualify.

### Filing Questions

**Q: Can I file ITR for previous years?** A: You can file a belated return for the past two
assessment years only.

**Q: What if I can't file by July 31st?** A: You can file a belated return until December 31st with
a late fee.

**Q: Do I need a CA to file my returns?** A: For simple salaried income, CA assistance is not
required. The online portal is user-friendly.

1. **Understand your salary structure** – Know what's taxable and what's exempt.

2. **Start tax planning from Day 1** – Don't wait until March to think about taxes.

## Planning Your Tax-Saving Investment Strategy

### Monthly Investment Approach

Instead of a lump-sum investment in March, consider:

| Category        | Monthly Amount | Annual Total |
| --------------- | -------------- | ------------ |
| EPF (automatic) | ₹3,000         | ₹36,000      |
| ELSS SIP        | ₹5,000         | ₹60,000      |
| PPF             | ₹2,000         | ₹24,000      |
| NPS (80CCD1B)   | ₹4,200         | ₹50,400      |

This creates discipline and avoids stress.

### Investment Priority Order

1. **EPF** – It's automatic and safe
2. **ELSS** – Shortest lock-in with growth potential
3. **Health Insurance (80D)** – Cover yourself and parents
4. **PPF** – For ultra-safe long-term savings
5. **NPS** – For additional deduction beyond 80C
